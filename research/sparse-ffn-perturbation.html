Can a small disturbance reveal a fragile answer?

A model can sound certain even when its answer is wrong. I investigated whether the stability of its internal feed-forward activations offers another clue—without changing the answer-generation policy.

Back to selected research ↗
Question → method → evidence

The question

Most uncertainty estimators look at outputs: token probabilities, alternative completions, or self-reported confidence. This study instead asks whether a generated answer remains stable when a small amount of noise is applied to intermediate feed-forward-network (FFN) activations. The working hypothesis is that fragile recall or reasoning may produce more variable predictions.

Timeline: this research was developed before the EMNLP 2026 ARR submission deadline of 25 May 2026. The results below are from a research manuscript, not a claim of conference acceptance or publication.

The method in plain language

First, the base model generates an answer normally. We then replay evaluation passes for its generated tokens, injecting Gaussian noise into a sparse selection of FFN intermediate activations. We compare the probabilities assigned to those fixed tokens across passes. A larger change means a stronger instability signal.

What the manuscript reports

In a Llama‑3.2‑1B evaluation, the proposed FFN-noise score separated correct from incorrect answers more effectively than the strongest comparison in the manuscript on Math500. A second, factual-recall-plus-arithmetic benchmark—Leg-Counting—also showed a strong discrimination result. Ablations suggest that where and how much noise is injected matter: dense or overly strong perturbations can damage the representation rather than reveal useful uncertainty.

What remains open

Internal instability is not automatically a decision rule. The probe still requires repeated forward passes, and the useful noise setting may depend on model architecture and task. The broader question is whether this kind of signal can be calibrated well enough to trigger a practical action—verification, clarification, or abstention—before an AI system commits to a risky answer.
